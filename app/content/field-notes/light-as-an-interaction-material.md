In a three-dimensional interface, light can do the same work that color, borders, and elevation do in a conventional product surface. It can identify the active object, separate layers of depth, and direct attention without adding another visible control.

The difference is that light feels environmental. The interface appears to react as a world rather than as a collection of components.

## Start with one dominant source

A scene becomes easier to read when one light establishes the primary direction. Secondary lights can recover detail or introduce color, but they should not compete for ownership of the form.

This is especially important with textured surfaces. Height maps and displacement only read clearly when light crosses them at an angle.

## Use glow as confirmation

Glow is most effective at the edge of an object or during a change of state. A constant, saturated halo quickly loses meaning and can erase the surface detail it was meant to frame.

For interactive planets, I prefer a narrow atmosphere with a muted color. The glow becomes visible during the camera approach, while the surface remains the main event.

## Let shadow preserve scale

Shadows provide evidence that objects occupy the same space. They also reveal distance: a moon crossing a planetary surface communicates orbit and scale more directly than a label can.

The strongest result is rarely the brightest one. When lighting carries information, restraint makes the signal clearer.
