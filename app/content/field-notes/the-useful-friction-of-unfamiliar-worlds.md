Familiar patterns are efficient. They let people move through a product without spending much attention on the interface itself. But total familiarity can also flatten an experience until nothing feels specific to the idea behind it.

The useful question is not whether an interaction is conventional. It is whether the extra attention it asks for creates enough value.

## Spend friction deliberately

I think of unfamiliar interaction as a limited budget. It is worth spending when it:

1. Makes an important relationship easier to understand.
2. Gives a brand or story a distinctive physical quality.
3. Turns a meaningful transition into something the user can feel.

It is usually not worth spending on routine tasks such as account recovery, forms, settings, or basic navigation labels.

## Pair novelty with evidence

An unfamiliar control needs immediate evidence that it is working. Cursor changes, responsive movement, concise labels, and visible destination states can teach the interaction before instructions are necessary.

The feedback should arrive at the same moment as the action. Delayed confirmation makes even a beautiful interaction feel unreliable.

## Keep the conventional route nearby

The most expressive version of an experience should not be the only version. Keyboard access, reduced-motion behavior, semantic document structure, and direct links keep the work usable outside its ideal cinematic conditions.

That fallback is not separate from the creative direction. It is what gives the creative direction room to take a real risk.
